Class #1Overview of financial accounting (9 Oct.)

Readings: Stolowy et al., ch. 1 to 5 (pages in the course outline). Workbook cases: Verdi, Busoni.

In one look.
  • = Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity
  • = Net income = Revenue − Expenses (included in equity)
  • Every transaction hits both sides by the same amount, or reclassifies within one side.
  • Revenue or expense only if equity moves, excluding contributions and dividends.
  • Net income ≠ cash (borrowing, equipment, credit sales).
  • Statements: balance sheet, income statement, cash flow statement, statement of changes in equity, notes.
  • Historical cost (verifiable) vs fair value (market); IFRS 18 replaces IAS 1 in 2027.

Why financial accounting matters

  • Accounting is the "language of business". Financial accounting reports the resources a company controls and how management uses them.
  • Users: shareholders, lenders, suppliers, customers, employees, analysts, the State, the public, management. Information that meets investors' needs is assumed to meet everyone's.
  • This course takes the user's perspective: read and interpret financial statements.

Key concepts · The business equation

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity Net income = Revenue - Expenses (net income stays inside equity)
TermCourse definition (IASB 2018 conceptual framework)
AssetA present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events; an economic resource is a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits. Current or non-current.
LiabilityA present obligation to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events. Current or non-current.
Shareholders' equityThe residual interest in the assets after deducting liabilities: share capital + retained earnings.
RevenueAn increase in assets or decrease in liabilities that increases equity, excluding shareholders' contributions.
ExpenseA decrease in assets or increase in liabilities that decreases equity, excluding distributions to shareholders.

Mechanism · How every transaction keeps the equation balanced

  1. Identify what the company receives and what it gives up or owes.
  2. Every transaction either changes both sides by the same amount (+X / +X or -X / -X) or reclassifies within one side (+X and -X).
  3. Then ask: does it touch shareholders' equity? If yes, it is revenue or an expense - unless it is a shareholders' contribution or a dividend.
Net income is not cash. Some transactions move cash without touching income (borrowing, buying equipment, repaying a loan); others hit income and cash in different periods (credit sales, credit purchases). That is the accruals principle.

The financial statements

  • Statement of financial position (balance sheet): uses of resources (assets) and their origins (liabilities and equity) at a point in time.
  • Income statement (statement of profit or loss): revenue - expenses over the period.
  • Statement of changes in equity, statement of cash flows (operating, investing, financing) and notes.
  • The statements are linked: net income feeds equity; the change in cash explains the cash line of the balance sheet.

The rules of the game

  • Why rules: to value assets and liabilities, recognize revenue and expenses, and separate an asset from an expense (a share bought for 5 worth 30 at year-end; a pool table sold on 31/12, delivered on 31/01; 60M of R&D).
  • Standard setters: the IASB (IAS/IFRS) and the ISSB (sustainability, IFRS S1 and S2, since 2021) within the IFRS Foundation; the EU ("IFRS as adopted by the EU"); the FASB (US GAAP); the ANC (French rules). US GAAP is more rules-based, IFRS more principles-based. The 1993 Daimler-Benz example: a profit of 615M DM under German rules, a loss of 1,839M DM under US GAAP.
  • Principles: going concern, faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from error), consistency, accruals, prudence, monetary unit.
  • Measurement bases: historical cost (easy, verifiable, but blind to market value) and current value, including fair value (market price; mostly financial instruments; IAS 16 allows revaluation, IAS 40 fair value for investment property).
  • Governance: shareholders elect the board, which appoints management and an audit committee; the auditor certifies the accounts.
2027 change (IFRS 18). IFRS 18 replaces IAS 1 for periods starting on or after 1 January 2027. The income statement is split into five categories (operating, investing, financing, tax, discontinued operations) with two subtotals: operating profit and profit before financing and tax. This course presents the 2026 statements.

Classic traps

  • Collecting a receivable or paying a payable does not touch income.
  • A loan is not revenue; repaying it is not an expense; only the interest is an expense.
  • A shareholders' contribution increases equity without being revenue.

Class #2Operating cycle and current assets (16 Oct.)

Readings: Stolowy et al., ch. 6, 9 and 10. Cases: Mozart (Workbook), Schultz exercise.

In one look.
  • IFRS 15: contract → obligations → price → allocation → revenue when the obligation is satisfied.
  • Revenue: goods at a point in time (transfer of control), services over time.
  • = Acquisition cost = price + transport + duties − discounts
  • = Cost of goods sold = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory
  • Rising prices: FIFO → higher income and inventory; LIFO → the opposite (banned under IFRS).
  • Inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
  • Doubtful receivable → impairment (expense); uncollectible → written off.

Key concepts · The operating cycle

Purchases → inventories → production → sales → receivables → collections; purchases → payables → payments. Current assets are used within one cycle and turn into cash within a year: inventories, trade receivables, tax receivables, advances to suppliers, cash.

Mechanism · Recognizing revenue and expenses

Accrual accountingCash accounting
RevenueWhen the performance obligation is satisfied (at a point in time or over time)When collected
ExpensesWhen incurredWhen paid
  1. IFRS 15 five-step model: identify the contract; identify the performance obligations; determine the transaction price; allocate it to the obligations; recognize revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied.
  2. Goods: at a point in time (transfer of control); services: over time. LVMH example: retail sales recognized when the customer buys, services as rendered, net of discounts.
  3. The ideal is to match expenses to the revenue they helped produce.
Revenue recognition is the fraud hotspot. In 60% of the US scandals studied by COSO, revenue recognition was manipulated: period shifts ("cookie jar") or fictitious sales (Gowex, Satyam). See also Tesco (2014).

Inventories

  • Until a product is sold, its costs stay on the balance sheet. Measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value.
  • Types: merchandise, raw materials, finished goods.

Accounts receivable

  • They exist because transactions are not settled instantly and accounting is accrual-based.

Class #3Investing cycle and non-current assets (23 Oct.)

Readings: Stolowy et al., ch. 7 and 8. Case: Amorti (Workbook).

In one look.
  • Non-current = used over more than one cycle (purpose, not nature).
  • Entry cost = price + directly attributable costs (100 + 5 + 15 + 30 = 150).
  • = Straight-line = (cost − residual value) / useful life
  • = Double declining balance = carrying amount × 2 / useful life
  • = Gain on disposal = sale proceeds − carrying amount
  • Land, brands and goodwill: no depreciation. Advertising and research: expensed.

Key concepts · Current vs non-current

  • A non-current asset is not meant to be turned into cash within the normal cycle (usually one year). The difference with a current asset is use: the same car is a fixed asset for one company and inventory for a car dealer.

Mechanism · Initial recognition at cost

  1. Start from the acquisition cost (if bought) or production cost (if built).
  2. Add the directly attributable costs needed to bring the asset to working condition: site preparation, delivery, installation, architects' and engineers' fees, estimated dismantling costs.
  3. Slide example: price 100 + customs 5 + transport 15 + fees 30 = equipment at 150.
  • Intangibles: identifiable non-monetary assets without physical substance, recognized if future benefits are probable and the cost is measurable (e.g. development costs). No asset for internally generated brands, internal goodwill, customer lists, advertising, training or research. Only acquired goodwill is recognized (price paid - fair value of net identifiable assets; LVMH and Tiffany in 2021).

Mechanism · Depreciation

Depreciable amount = cost - residual value Straight-line: rate = 1 / useful life; annual charge = depreciable amount x rate Declining balance (DDBM): rate = 2 x straight-line rate; charge = net book value x rate Net book value = cost - accumulated depreciation (- impairment) Gain or loss on disposal = sale price - net book value
  • Partial year: pro-rate the charge (months or days).
  • Accelerated methods charge more in early years (cars, ovens) and bring a tax shield. In the US the multiple is often 2; in continental Europe the tax authorities set coefficients by useful life.

Class #4Financing cycle, equity and liabilities (30 Oct.)

Readings: Stolowy et al., ch. 11 and 12. Cases: ACME (Workbook), Gerry Co. exercise.

In one look.
  • = Share capital = shares × par value
  • = Share premium = (issue price − par value) × shares issued
  • = Book value per share = equity / shares outstanding
  • Appropriation: reserves, dividends (a liability once declared but unpaid), retained earnings.
  • Debt: repayment, accrued and deductible interest; equity: no repayment, control.

Key concepts · Equity vocabulary

Share capital = number of shares x par value Book value per share = shareholders' equity / number of shares Market capitalization = number of shares x share price Share premium = (issue price - par value) x new shares issued
  • Par value only defines the number of shares in the legal capital and each shareholder's power. Par ≠ book value ≠ market value.
  • The share premium (additional paid-in capital, capital surplus) records what new shareholders pay above the legal capital; it belongs to equity.

Mechanism · Issuing new shares

  1. New shares x par value → increases share capital.
  2. New shares x (issue price - par value) → increases share premium.
  3. New shares x issue price → cash coming in.

Profit appropriation

  • Three destinations: reserves (legal, statutory, free), dividends, retained earnings.
  • Three cases: keep everything in reserves, distribute everything, or a mix. Dividends decided but not yet paid become a liability (dividends payable).
  • The balance sheet is presented before appropriation (the year's income on its own line) or after (reserves updated, dividends in liabilities).

Liabilities

A liability is a present obligation to transfer an economic resource as a result of a past event (legally enforceable or customarily accepted; settled in cash, goods, services, shares…).

Class #5End-of-Year adjustments (6 Nov.)

Readings: Stolowy et al., ch. 4, 7 and 12. Cases: Flossie Flowers, Imp, Provisor (Workbook), Offenbach exercise.

In one look.
  • Prepaid expense (asset), deferred revenue (liability), accrued expense (liability), accrued revenue (asset).
  • = Recoverable amount = max(fair value − costs of disposal; value in use)
  • = Impairment loss = carrying amount − recoverable amount
  • Provision: present obligation + probable outflow + reliable estimate; otherwise a contingent liability in the notes.
  • No provision for future losses; restructuring only once a formal plan is announced.

Why year-end adjustments

Depreciation and impairment, accruals and deferrals, provisions, doubtful receivables: they apply the accruals, matching and prudence principles, cutting activity into annual periods at the closing date (often 31 December). The slides show a company can be very profitable with dangerously low cash (clients paying at two months, suppliers at one), or loss-making with rising cash.

Mechanism · Impairment of assets

Recoverable amount = max(fair value - costs of disposal; value in use) Impairment loss = carrying amount - recoverable amount (if positive)
  • Prudence: an asset may not sit on the books above its recoverable amount. Value in use = present value of the asset's expected cash flows.
  • One-off test, usually at closing. Reversal allowed if the recoverable amount rises again (IAS 36.114), capped at what the carrying amount would have been without impairment; never for goodwill.

Class #6Statement of cash flows (13 Nov.)

Readings: Stolowy et al., ch. 16. Case: Beethoven (Workbook).

In one look.
  • = Change in cash = operating + investing + financing cash flows
  • Direct: receipts − payments; indirect: adjusted net income.
  • = Indirect: net income + depreciation − Δ receivables and inventory + Δ operating payables
  • Until 2026: choice for interest and dividends; from 2027: received → investing, paid → financing.

Key concept · What the statement explains

The statement of cash flows explains the change in cash and cash equivalents (very liquid short-term investments with negligible risk of value change) over the period.

Operating cash flows (I) + Investing cash flows (II) = investment inflows - outflows + Financing cash flows (III) = financing inflows - outflows = Net change in cash (IV) Closing cash = opening cash + IV

The three activities

ActivityInflowsOutflows
OperatingSales of goods and services, royalties, commissionsPurchases, wages and social charges, taxes
InvestingDisposals of fixed assets, repayments of loans grantedPurchases of fixed assets, loans granted, securities (not cash equivalents)
FinancingShare issues, borrowingsShare buybacks, loan repayments
Interest and dividends. Until 2026 (IAS 7), a choice: interest and dividends received in operating or investing; paid in operating or financing; tax in operating. From 1 January 2027 (IAS 7 amended by IFRS 18), for most entities: received in investing, paid in financing, tax in operating. In the exam, read the classification instructions.

Mechanism · Direct and indirect methods

  • Direct: collections from customers minus payments to suppliers, employees, etc. (from actual cash movements).
  • Indirect: start from net income and correct it. A semi-direct variant corrects sales by the change in receivables, etc. Both methods give the same figure and concern only operating flows.
  • From 2027, the indirect method starts from operating profit.
Indirect method: Net income + depreciation, amortization and impairment charges, - reversals (non-cash items) - gains / + losses on disposals (remove disposal results) - increase in inventories and receivables (+ if decrease) + increase in trade and operating payables (- if decrease) = Operating cash flow

Class #7Introduction to Management Accounting; costing terms and purpose (20 Nov.)

Readings: Horngren, ch. 2, 10 and 3. Cases: Kuniyakino, Montvert, Walk-About. Moodle self-assessment quiz: "The role of management accounting".

In one look.
  • Costs variable or fixed (by volume); direct or indirect (by cost object).
  • = Unit contribution margin = price − variable cost; margin rate = margin / price
  • = Break-even point = fixed costs / unit contribution margin
  • = Target volume = (fixed costs + target income) / unit contribution margin
  • Decide with relevant costs (future, differential); ignore sunk costs.
  • Scarce resource: margin per unit of the resource.

Key concepts · What management accounting is

  • It measures and communicates financial and non-financial information to help managers decide (launch a product, accept a client, make or buy…) and shape strategy (cost leadership or differentiation).
  • Financial accounting: record, report outside, mandatory and standardized, annual, historical. Management accounting: specific to each organization, not mandatory, forward-looking, at any frequency (month, week, ad hoc). Management control uses this information to steer strategy and operations (budgets, variances, performance measurement).

Cost terms

  • Cost: a resource sacrificed to reach a goal. Cost object: anything whose cost we want to measure (brand, range, client, project, activity…).
  • Not all costs are expenses and vice versa: an investment is neither (cash down, asset up); depreciation is both a cost and an expense with no cash outflow.
DistinctionDefinitionKeep in mind
Variable / fixedVariable: varies in total with volume. Fixed: does not vary in total over the period despite volume.Depends on the contract and the horizon: the longer the horizon, the more costs are variable.

Use unit costs cautiously. Unit cost = total cost / number of units. Kruger: fixed rent 94,500 + handlebar 52 per bike. At 1,000 bikes: (94,500 + 52,000)/1,000 = 146.50. At 600 bikes: (94,500 + 31,200)/600 = 209.50. Using 146.50 would understate the cost.

Mechanism · The cost-volume-profit (CVP) model

Profit = P x Q - v x Q - FC Contribution margin per unit = P - v Contribution margin ratio = (P - v) / P Breakeven volume = FC / (P - v) Breakeven revenue = FC / margin ratio Volume for a target profit = (FC + target profit) / (P - v) Margin of safety = sales - breakeven sales
  • Assumptions: volume is the only driver; costs split into fixed and variable, linear within the relevant range; price, unit costs and fixed costs known and constant; one product or a constant mix; no time value of money.
  • Uses: business plans, feasibility, profitability and risk diagnosis. Limits: single driver, linearity, constant mix, the fixed/variable split depends on the horizon.

Relevant costs for decisions

  • Relevant costs and revenues: future costs and revenues that differ between options. Historical costs only help forecast; sunk costs are irrelevant.
  • Do not assume all variable costs are relevant, nor that all fixed costs are irrelevant. Full unit costs can mislead (irrelevant costs included; same unit cost at different volumes).
  • Opportunity cost: the contribution given up by not using a limited resource in its best alternative use; never recorded in accounting.
  • Scarce resource: at full capacity, rank products by contribution per unit of the scarce resource, not per unit of product.
  • Pricing: cost is only one input (competition, clients' ability to pay, perceived value); separate special sales from the regular price.
Noel (towels): capacity 44,000, production 30,000; variable 8.50 (6.50 + 0.50 + 1.50); fixed 150,000. Full cost (8.5×30,000 + 150,000)/30,000 = 13.50. A special order of 5,000 at 11.50: only variable costs change → contribution 3 × 5,000 = +15,000, accept (spare capacity exists).

Class #8Cost allocation: job order costing and process-costing (27 Nov.)

Readings: Horngren, ch. 4, 5 and 14. Cases: My New Car, Immoservices, Alp'Ski (part 1), WattWatt.

In one look.
  • = Cost per unit of work = cost centre cost / units of the allocation base
  • Job costing (by order) vs process costing (mass production).
  • Support centres: direct or sequential method.
  • Full cost is no basis for deciding to drop a product.

Why allocate indirect costs

  • Direct costs are traced; indirect costs are shared resources that must be allocated to build a full cost (pricing, portfolio strategy, cost reduction, full-cost inventory valuation).
Careful: never use a full cost to drop a product or outsource - reason with differential costs and revenues instead.

Key concepts

Full cost = traced direct costs + allocated indirect costs Cost per allocation unit = cost pool total / number of units of the allocation base Indirect cost charged = units consumed x cost per allocation unit
  • Cost pool: a grouping of indirect costs. Allocation base (cost driver, unite d'oeuvre): a measurable variable (hours, kg, euros, m2…) causally linked to the pool's costs. A homogeneous pool gathers costs with the same cause.
  • Choosing the driver: economic plausibility, quality of fit, regression slope.
  • Support pools (IT, HR, R&D, maintenance) vs operating pools (purchasing, production, distribution).

Job-costing steps: identify the job; its direct costs; the relevant indirect pools; each pool's allocation base; the cost per allocation unit; then charge the job.

Mechanism · Allocating support pools

  1. Direct method: only services to operating pools count (simplest, most used).
  2. Step-down method: allocate first the support pool that serves the other support pools the most, then the next, with no return.
  3. Reciprocal method: all cross-services (equations); off syllabus. The French "centres d'analyse" method (PCG 1947) uses it.

Class #9Activity-Based Costing (4 Dec.)

Readings: Horngren, ch. 6 and 7. Cases: Alp'Ski (part 2), McCarthy, Castelo Branco.

In one look.
  • ABC: indirect costs → activities → products via cost drivers.
  • = Cost per driver = activity cost / total drivers
  • Reveals the true cost of complex products (Alp'Ski, McCarthy, Stella Cuero).
  • Limit: heavy database; shifts costs between products.

Key concepts · Why ABC

  • Born in the US in the 1980s, when a single cost pool was no longer enough. Pools are multiplied to stay homogeneous and split by activities rather than by organizational departments.
  • No more support vs operating pools: every activity is charged to cost objects according to its consumption of drivers.

Mechanism · Implementing ABC

  1. Define the cost objects; (2) their direct costs; (3) the activities, their costs and their driver; compute the cost per driver; (4) charge according to driver consumption.
Cost per driver = activity cost / total number of drivers Cost charged to a product = Sum(drivers consumed x cost per driver)

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths: faithful modeling (especially of complexity: number of products, components), traceability, well suited to services and processes.
  • Limits: a heavy database (drivers x products) to build and maintain, risk of bureaucracy.
  • From ABC to ABM (activity-based management): cut costs, improve processes, plan activities, set prices and mix, think about customer value. About 40% of US companies use ABC, 20-35% in Europe and France.
Cutting the number of drivers of a product changes the split between products, not necessarily the company's total cost.

Class #10Introduction to budget and variance analysis (11 Dec.)

Readings: Horngren, ch. 7 and 8. Cases: Smell Good, Puyricard, Castelo Branco. A mock exam is announced after this class.

In one look.
  • Budget: a quantified expression of a forward-looking vision over a limited period.
  • = Flexible budget = budgeted unit prices and costs × actual volume
  • = Sales-volume variance = flexible budget − static budget
  • = Flexible-budget variance = actual − flexible budget

Key concepts · What a budget is

  • A quantified expression, over a limited period (usually one year), of a forward-looking view of the company, with financial and non-financial items. A reference point for action. Master budget and departmental budgets; no standard template; most are built on a projected income statement.
  • Approach: strategy → objectives → action plans → resources, from past performance and forecasts.
  • It sits in a wider process: strategic plan (3-5 years), capital budgets, operational plans (1-3 years), annual budget, departmental budgets (sales, production, materials, labor, overheads, cash, financing).

Common problems and control

  • Imposed without managers, a budget looks unrealistic and is little used; built with them, it takes time and triggers negotiations.
  • Control: compare budget and actual, adjust forecasts; this is the pivot of performance and variance analysis.

Mechanism · Variance analysis (sample exam "A la Coque")

Flexible budget = budget recomputed at the actual volume, with budgeted prices and unit costs Total (static budget) variance = actual - static budget Volume variance = flexible budget - static budget Flexible budget variance = actual - flexible budget A variance is favorable if it increases profit

§Key terms

TermDefinition
AssetPresent economic resource controlled as a result of past events.
LiabilityPresent obligation to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events.
Shareholders' equityResidual interest in assets after deducting liabilities.
Revenue / expenseEquity-changing flows, excluding contributions and distributions.
AccrualsRevenue and expenses are recognized when earned or incurred, not when cash moves.
MatchingExpenses are tied to the revenue they helped produce.
FIFO / LIFO / WACInventory cost-flow assumptions: first-in-first-out, last-in-first-out (US GAAP only), weighted average cost.
Depreciation / amortization / depletionSystematic consumption of PP&E, intangibles and natural resources.
Net book valueCost - accumulated depreciation - impairment.
ImpairmentCarrying amount above recoverable amount = max(fair value - costs of disposal, value in use).
ProvisionLiability of uncertain timing or amount: present obligation, probable outflow, reliable estimate.
Prepaid / accrued expensePaid before it relates (asset) / relates before it is paid (liability).
Deferred / accrued revenueCollected before it is earned (liability) / earned before it is collected (asset).
Contribution marginPrice - variable cost per unit; breakeven = fixed costs / unit margin.
Relevant costFuture cost that differs between options; sunk costs are never relevant.
Cost pool / allocation baseGrouping of indirect costs / measurable driver used to allocate them.
Job vs process costingCost per order or batch / average cost of identical units.
